Understanding the Information Bottleneck

posted 06/16/2026

Draft version; this page is a work in progress.

At first glance, it might be unclear why information theory has resurged as a topic of discussion within machine learning circles. Information theory tells us the most efficient way to represent data given our knowledge about the process that creates it, often leveraging general statistical properties that might be useful in, say, noisy audio signals, but seem less immediately applicable to modeling or complex reasoning.

More specifically, information theory tells us about the most efficient encoding $c(X)$ of a random variable $X$ taking values in a set $\mathcal{X}$. If we know certain things about $X$, e.g. that it is uniform, independent and identically distributed, or generated by Markov chains themselves satisfying certain properties, then we can say certain things about the informational content of our data and its representation.

This is actually saying something quite remarkable: if we know roughly how something works (or how it looks like it works), we can efficiently represent it. A big idea within modern machine learning is that maybe this relationship holds conversely: if we can efficiently represent some data, then those representations must in some way leverage the underlying structure of the process generating that data. Depending on how formal and complex that structure is, leveraging it might genuinely constitute some form of intelligent behavior.

3b1b has an excellent video series on this notion, that compression is intelligence. This page is meant to demonstrate one specific interpretation of that notion: a formalization of intelligence in information-theoretic concepts, as a tradeoff between compression and relevance. This is the information bottleneck.

This page walks through some basic concepts in information theory while largely following four papers:

  • Tishby, Pereira, Bialek (1999): The information bottleneck method
  • Tishby, Zaslavsky (2015): Deep Learning and the Information Bottleneck Principle
  • Wu, Ren, Li, Leskovec (2020): Graph Information Bottleneck
  • Yuan et al. (2024): Dynamic Graph Information Bottleneck

My goal here is to convey a high-level intuition of the relevant information-theoretic concepts, explain the information bottleneck and what it says about intelligence, and demonstrate its effectiveness in graph models, which are notorious for overfitting.

Primer

The information bottleneck relies on three quantities. The entropy $H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)$ is the most foundational of them, and measures uncertainty. Entropy is maximized by a uniform distribution: when all outcomes are equally likely, uncertainty is maximized. The entropy also tells us how many bits (on average) it takes for an optimal encoder to specify an outcome of $X$.

The interactive below aims to give you a sense for how entropy (and thus uncertainty) track uniformity:

entropy and uniformity

We can also quantify more nuanced notions of uncertainty: conditional entropy $H(X \mid Y)$ is the uncertainty that remains about $X$ after observing some (possibly related) $Y$; if $Y$ has nothing to do with $X$ (i.e. they are independent) then $H(X \mid Y) = H(X)$, otherwise, we'll always learn something, so $H(X \mid Y) < H(X)$. The difference here between the conditional and 'regular' entropy is the most important quantity for the information bottleneck, the mutual information $I(X;Y)$, which quantifies the uncertainty removed about $X$ after observing some $Y$:

$$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} = D\!\left[\,p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\,\right]$$

Mutual information is measured by the (Kullback-Leibler) divergence between the probabilities of (a) seeing both events at the same time and (b) seeing them at the same time, assuming they were independent. So if both events were really independent, then the "difference" between what you saw and what you would have seen were they actually independent is zero, and there isn't any mutual information between them. By contrast, dependent events would have higher mutual information, since their joint distribution would differ a fair bit from the joint distribution were they independent.

Divergence is a weird notion of "distance" or "difference" to wrap your head around, though--for starters, it's not symmetric, and it scales somewhat unintuitively. The interactive below shows how it behaves for two Gaussians.

One last ingredient: as we model increasingly complex processes--including, later, the layers of a neural network itself--we will make use of Markov chains, systems that whose transitions between states are specified by transition probabilities, and where the future depends on the present only through the current state. When random variables form such a chain, written $A \to B \to C$, we mean that $C$ depends on $A$ only through $B$.

This Markov structure immediately gives the data processing inequality (DPI): $I(A;B) \ge I(A;C)$; processing can only destroy information. This single inequality does lots of heavy lifting for everything that follows.

The information bottleneck

Classic lossy compression is rate-distortion theory: we want to compress $X$ into a representation $\tilde{X}$ using as few bits as possible (e.g. with a small rate $I(X; \tilde{X})$), while keeping a distortion $\langle d(x,\tilde{x})\rangle$ small. The catch is that in rate-distorition theory we need to supply the distortion function $d$, which means we need to choose which features of the signal matter. For speech, images, or protein sequences, nobody actually knows the "correct" $d$.

The information bottleneck aims to avoid defining relevance by hand, instead defining it through labels $Y$ that we have samples of. Relevant information is then just $I(X; Y)$, and a good representation is a compressed $\tilde{X}$ that keeps as much of it as possible. As an example, consider the following toy problem. We have $\vert X \vert = 8$ signals, each with its own relevance profile $p(y \mid x)$ over a binary label $Y \in \{0, 1\}$, and a bottleneck of $\vert \tilde{X} \vert = 3$ codewords to compress them into. The signals fall into three natural groups by their $p(y \mid x)$ — low, medium, and high $p(y=1\mid x)$. At low $\beta$, everything collapses to one codeword (maximal compression, zero relevance); as $\beta$ grows the encoder splits into 2, then 3 clusters. Those splits are second-order phase transitions in $\beta$, referred to as bifurcations in the information plane.
remark (bifurcations) The information curve is concave with slope $\delta I(\tilde{X};Y)/\delta I(X;\tilde{X}) = \beta^{-1}$. Annealing $\beta$ from 0 upward traces the curve; the cluster splits ("phase transitions") visible in the heatmap happen at critical $\beta$ values determined by second-order statistics of the joint distribution. This is important: the IB litereature claims that these bifurcations have something to say about network architecture.

Deep learning and the information bottleneck

A feedforward network maps inputs $X$ through hidden layers $h_1, \dots, h_m$ to a prediction $\hat{Y}$. Each layer only sees the previous one, so the layers form a Markov chain. By the DPI we get the chain of inequalities:

$$I(Y;X) \;\ge\; I(Y;h_1) \;\ge\; \cdots \;\ge\; I(Y;h_m) \;\ge\; I(Y;\hat{Y})$$
remark (information loss) Notice that relevant information lost at any layer is gone forever; no amount of downstream "cleverness" recovers it.

Under the information bottleneck, we treat each layer $h_i$ as a bottleneck variable, place it on the information plane at coordinates $(I(X;h_i),\, I(Y;h_i))$, and compare the whole network against the optimal IB curve for the data's joint distribution $p(X,Y)$. Supervised learning, in this view, is the IB problem: our goal is to extract an approximate minimal sufficient statistic of $X$ for $Y$.

remark (cross-entropy) It turns out that the relevance half $I(Y;Z)$ of the objective is something we already optimize every day. Though relevance cannot be computed directly (it needs the true $p(y \mid z)$), we can lower-bound it by sticking a classifier $q(y \mid z)$ on top of the representation and asking how well it predicts the labels. Maximizing that bound is exactly minimizing the cross-entropy loss. In other words, the "maximize relevance" half of the information bottleneck is just ordinary supervised training in disguise; the only genuinely new ingredient deep IB adds is the compression half, $\beta\, I(X;Z)$, which shows up as a regularizer.

Why would we want layers to compress (i.e. minimize $I(X;h_i)$), rather than just maximize relevance? The answer lies in generalization: the IB authors show that, by finite-sample bounds, the estimation error of $I(\tilde{X};Y)$ scales at $O(K|\mathcal{Y}|/\sqrt{n})$ where $K \approx 2^{I(X;\tilde{X})}$ is the effective description length of the representation. A layer that hasn't compressed is a layer whose apparent relevance you can't trust, as it may be overfitting. Again, notice this is making a rather bold statement: compression is necessary for generalization.

So what does this predict about training? If each layer is racing toward a minimal sufficient statistic, its position $(I(X;h_i),\, I(Y;h_i))$ on the information plane should not sit still during optimization. Empirical information-plane studies report a surprising two-phase trajectory for stochastic gradient descent: first, a brief fitting phase, where every layer rapidly gains information about both $X$ and $Y$ as the network memorizes the training signal. Then, a much longer compression phase, where the layers slowly shed information about the input ($I(X;h)$) while holding onto relevance ($I(Y;h)$). In this picture, most of training is spent forgetting the irrelevant parts of $X$ rather than fitting the labels.

Notice that the gap between the layers and optimal curve (which the authors call $\Delta G$) is the unrecoverable relevance due to finite samples. Deeper layers compress further (smaller $I(X;h) \implies$ smaller $\beta$) and, by the DPI, sit no higher in $I(Y;h)$.
remark (training dynamics) This is not a settled theory; the dynamics-of-training claims remain contested. Two-phase compression was reported empirically in Schwartz-Ziv & Tishby 2017, but later work (notably Saxe et al., 2018) showed that the compression phase can depend on the choice of activation function and on how mutual information is estimated/binned in deterministic networks (where $I(X;h)$ is technically infinite or constant unless you add noise or discretize).
remark (model architecture) The 2015 paper also conjectures a correspondence between the IB curve's bifurcations and where layers "should" sit: phase transitions on the curve are governed by second-order correlations of the data, the same structure that breaks linear separability. Since a single sigmoidal neuron can only carve linearly separable regions, optimal layer placements would sit just past the bifurcations. This idea remains contested; for more see Tishby's remarks.

Graph information bottleneck

So far, the information bottleneck has assumed that training examples are i.i.d., and that inputs are feature vectors. Graphs break both of these assumptions: input is now $\mathcal{D} = (A, X)$ (adjacency matrix & node features), and the rows of $X$ are correlated through the edges $E$, so we cannot split the data per node. Instead, we operate in a transductive setting, where we effectively observe a single realization of $P(\mathcal{D})$. The graph information bottleneck keeps the objective in a recognizable form

$$\min_{\;\mathbb{P}(Z\,\mid\,\mathcal{D})\,\in\,\Omega}\ \mathrm{GIB}_{\beta}(\mathcal{D}, Y; Z) \;\triangleq\; -I(Y;Z) \;+\; \beta\, I(\mathcal{D};Z)$$

but searches instead over $\Omega$ (the space of all encoders). GIB deals with two obstacles:

  • First, our data are no longer i.i.d., which breaks most of the fundamental assumptions of information theory. GIB replaces the i.i.d. assumption with a local-dependence assumption: conditioned on its $k$-hop neighborhood, a node is independent of the rest of the graph. This restricts $\Omega$ to encoders that respect local dependence, which take the form of layered Markov chains that alternate between extracting structural information $Z_A^{(l)}$ and feature information $Z_X^{(l)}$.
  • Second, $A$ is discrete, which throws a wrench in our differentiation plans. To resolve this, the encoder treats attention weights as parameters of a neighbor-sampling distribution (e.g. categorical or Bernoulli), with Gumbel-softmax reparameterization.

Now we need to actually compute the objective. Just like in deep learning, the compression term $I(\mathcal{D};Z)$ is intractable, so we replace it with a variational upper bound (an expression we can compute that is guaranteed to be at least as big). The new wrinkle is that our encoder builds $Z$ in two interleaved pieces (structure & features), so the bound splits into two matching pieces. By the DPI we have:

$$I(\mathcal{D}; Z^{(L)}_X) \;\le\; \sum_{l \in S_A} \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\tfrac{\mathbb{P}(Z_A^{(l)} \mid A,\, Z_X^{(l-1)})}{\mathbb{Q}(Z_A^{(l)})}\right]}_{\mathrm{AIB}^{(l)}\;\text{(structure)}} \;+\; \sum_{l \in S_X} \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\tfrac{\mathbb{P}(Z_X^{(l)} \mid Z_X^{(l-1)},\, Z_A^{(l)})}{\mathbb{Q}(Z_X^{(l)})}\right]}_{\mathrm{XIB}^{(l)}\;\text{(features)}}$$

Each term is a KL divergence between what the encoder actually does and a deliberately dumb reference distribution $\mathbb{Q}$: for structure (the AIB term), $\mathbb{Q}$ treats every neighbor as equally worth attending to; for features (the XIB term), $\mathbb{Q}$ is a generic blob (a mixture of Gaussians). Measuring against a dumb baseline is what makes these terms penalties: the encoder only pays when it pays attention to specific edges or specific feature directions, so each term taxes the model for using structure or features it didn't strictly need.

remark (implementation) Notice that a GIB model is just a graph attention network (GAT) trained with the usual cross-entropy loss, plus two regularizers, one taxing edges, one taxing features. The bottleneck manifests entirely in the loss function.
remark (adversarial attacks) Splitting the penalty into two terms allows us to defend against different kinds of adversarial attacks. Attackers can poison either the edges (adding fake connections) or the features (corrupting node attributes). Against edge-based attacks, the structure penalty does nearly all the work, while against feature noise the feature penalty kicks in. Notice also that, because the network passes messages over a resampled version of the graph rather than the original adjacency matrix, it never fully trusts the raw edges in the first place, allowing for additional robustness.

Dynamic graph information bottleneck

DGIB extends the GIB to dynamic graphs: a sequence of discrete snapshots $\mathcal{G}^1, \dots, \mathcal{G}^T$ under the link prediction task. A naive adaptation (running GIB per snapshot, aggregating with an RNN, and plugging $\mathcal{D} = (\mathcal{G}^{1:T}, X^{T+1})$ into the IB objective) is insufficient: the training process effectively decomposes into $T{+}1$ per-step Markov chains, and the network lazily satisfies each one; every intermediate $Z^{t+1}$ becomes minimal-and-sufficient for its own next step, while the final $Z^{T+1}$ (the one we actually care about) ends up sub-optimal. Per-step compression doesn't agree on what to keep.

The fix is a third requirement on top of minimal and sufficient: consensus. The compression at every step should preserve the predictive pattern shared across steps. DGIB enforces this by splitting the objective into two cooperating channels:

Making this trainable reuses GIB's playbook, just stretched along the time axis. Each of the three pieces gets handled the same way as before:

  • Relevance ($I(Y^{T+1};Z^{T+1})$) is still intractable, so we still slap a classifier $Q(Y \mid Z)$ on top; the bound collapses to ordinary cross-entropy loss.
  • Compression ($I(\mathcal{D};Z^{T+1})$): GIB's local-dependence assumption now extends across time, so a node depends only on its $k$-hop neighbors in the current and previous snapshot. That Markov structure lets us split the bound into per-snapshot pieces, summed over time: a structure penalty for the sampled edges $\hat{A}^t$ and a feature penalty for the features $Z^t$. These are exactly the two penalties from the graph section, now once per snapshot.
  • Consensus ($I(Z^{1:T};Z^{T+1})$) gets the same kind of variational upper bound as the compression term, but measured against the earlier representations instead of the raw input, penalizing the final representation for disagreeing with the ones before it.

remark (robustness) The same $\beta$ trade-off is still here at the end of the chain. DGIB's experiments show that dialing down the compression (larger $1/\beta_1, 1/\beta_2$) steadily improves accuracy on clean data but steadily hurts robustness under attack.

Closing thoughts

The information bottleneck is a playbook: pick a relevance variable, write down $\min\, I(\text{input};\,\text{representation}) - \beta\, I(\text{representation};\,\text{relevance})$, and then fight for tractability. Each paper just picks its fight in a different setting. The original IB could afford to be exact, solving the objective directly with a convergent set of self-consistent equations. Deep learning gave that up for variational bounds, which turn the relevance term into cross-entropy and the compression term into a divergence penalty. Graphs added the wrinkle that data points are no longer independent, so i.i.d. is swapped for local dependence and the compression penalty splits along structure and features. Dynamic graphs stretch that across time and add a consensus term so the snapshots don't each compress toward their own private solution.

But throughout all this, one idea keeps recurring: intelligence is a trade-off between compression and relevance.
